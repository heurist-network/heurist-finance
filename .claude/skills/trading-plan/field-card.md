# The Trader's Field Card

> A plan is a letter from your best self to your worst self.
> This note is that letter. It was written on a calm day, with a clear head.
>
> **Read Part 1 before every entry. Read Part 2 the moment your pulse is up. Read Part 3 after every close.**

---

## Part 1 — Before you enter (pre-flight)

**You are the casino, not the gambler.**
The house edge on roulette is ~5%. The casino loses spins all night long, to cheering crowds, and does not care — and it never changes the rules of roulette because of one spin. Your edge, if you have one, collects over *volume*. Any single trade is one spin. Build the rules with a clear head, then let the volume do the work.

Before clicking, write down (one line each — if you can't, don't trade):

1. **Thesis** — why this position makes money, in one sentence.
2. **The clock** — what timeframe does this thesis live on? Hours, days, weeks, months? *You may only consume information on that clock.* A weeks-trade watched on the minutes-chart is how anxiety gets a steering wheel.
3. **Exit prices** — target and stop. The stop amount is **tuition you have already agreed to pay**. It is a business expense, like a restaurant's food costs. If it hits, the system worked.
4. **The invalidation list** — *which specific news* would kill this thesis (e.g. for a memory-chip position: DRAM pricing rolls over, hyperscaler capex cuts, bad guidance on earnings day). Everything not on this list — wars, elections, Fed noise, red tape — is **pre-declared noise that the stop already handles**.
5. **The size test** — *"If a scary headline prints at 11pm, will I need to check this position?"* If yes → halve it. Still yes → halve again. **Calm is a sizing decision, not a personality trait.** You cannot think your way to calm in a position that's too big; you can size your way to calm in almost any position.
6. **Pre-fight the bad day** — somewhere during this trade there WILL be a red day, a scary headline, a paper drawdown. Decide *now* what you'll do: **nothing, unless the invalidation list triggered.** You have now already fought that battle. When the day comes, you only have to remember that you won it.

Then: set alerts at your decision prices, and **close the chart**. A position with no pending decision does not need your eyes.

## Part 2 — RED CARD: read this when you are scared, mid-trade

Stop. Before you touch anything, read all of this.

**1. Name what's happening.**
Fear is a body state, not information. Your pulse went up; the thesis did not change because your pulse did. The screen is not asking you a question right now — your adrenaline is.

**2. Run the three tests. A mid-trade change must pass ALL three.**

- **Test 1 — Is this already priced into the plan?**
  The stop exists precisely to answer *"what if something bad happens?"* You already paid for that insurance. A scary event is not a new category — it's an *instance* of the category your stop was bought for. Exiting early = paying for insurance, then jumping out the window anyway.

- **Test 2 — Does this news pass through the THESIS, or only through your pulse?**
  Ask literally: does this event change the supply, demand, or earnings of what I hold?
  - *A war headline in the Middle East* while holding semis → touches risk appetite for hours, touches the thesis not at all → **noise, the stop handles it.**
  - *"China blockades Taiwan"* while holding semis → travels straight through the thesis → **act, plan or no plan.**
  News must touch the thesis to touch the position. That is the whole rule.

- **Test 3 — Who is deciding right now?**
  The plan was written by calm-you: full analysis, no adrenaline, thinking on the trade's clock. The person at the screen right now is scared-you: minutes-chart, heart rate up, sample size of one. These are two different people and the second one is worse at this job. **The bar for letting the worse-qualified one override the better-qualified one is thesis-breaking news — not new feelings.**

**3. The rule of rules.**
Whatever you're about to do impulsively — turn it into a sentence. *"Exit any position when a scary geopolitical headline prints."* Now ask: would you write that rule into your system and apply it 100 times? (That one systematically sells fear-lows. It loses.) **If it's not good enough to be a rule, it's not good enough to do once.**

**4. Remember how this failure always looks.**
The plan was fine — entry, target, stop all set. Then a scary headline printed, and the position got dumped at flat or worse, nowhere near the stop. The market shrugged the headline off. The failure was never the plan — it was that the exit criterion silently changed from *"price hits the stop"* to *"I feel scared."* And remember the harder half of that lesson: **the exit is wrong even in the histories where the market DOES crash** — because it wasn't in the plan. That's not hindsight talking; that's process talking.

**5. Now do the physical thing.**
Close the chart. Stand up. Walk. Water. Re-check on the trade's clock — in an hour, or tomorrow — not in ten seconds. The market will still be there. Your edge only exists if you're still following the system when you come back.

**Every exception teaches your brain that the plan is negotiable — even the exceptions that get paid.** The precedent always costs more than the loss.

## Part 2.5 — Which market am I in? (route the alarm, don't suppress it)

Fear trained in one market misfires in another. A reflex learned where assets can actually die — speculative tokens, meme stocks, frauds — will scream *get out* in markets where dips are noise around a rising earnings stream. The reflex isn't wrong; it's answering for the wrong asset class. Before obeying the alarm, find the holding's row:

| Regime | Can it go to ~zero? | A dip usually means | The alarm is… |
|---|---|---|---|
| No-earnings speculative assets (speculative tokens, meme names) | yes | it's dying | **usually right** — dips are information |
| Crowded story stocks (high multiple, leverage behind the narrative) | effectively (−70–90%, may never recover) | often information | **mostly right** — check the narrative, fast |
| Profitable cyclicals | no, but −50–70% cyclically | the cycle may be turning | **half right** — route it to the invalidation list, not the sell button |
| Megacap quality / broad index | no | noise around rising earnings | **false positive** — thank it and hold |

Two rules of the ladder:

- It's never one asset class against another. It's the **floor** (are there earnings under this thing?) and the **drift** (does time work for or against the holder?). One market's survival reflex is another market's systematic sell-the-low.
- Same reflex, inverted payoff: where assets can die, selling the panic saves you; where earnings grow, selling the panic sells the low of a thing that recovers. Know which market you're standing in **before** you obey the alarm — then run the three tests above.

## Part 3 — After the trade (how to score it)

Grade the **decision**, never the result:

| | Good outcome | Bad outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Followed plan** | Deserved win ✅ | **Bad luck — still a GOOD trade** ✅ |
| **Broke plan** | **Dumb luck — the WORST trade** ⚠️ | Deserved loss ❌ |

- A decision is right or wrong **at the moment it's made**, with the information available then. What the market does at 3pm cannot reach back and change the quality of the 11am decision. Same decision → same grade, in every alternate history.
- **Followed plan + lost money = good trade.** Tuition paid, system intact.
- **Broke plan + made money = the most dangerous trade you can have.** It pays you to repeat the mistake.
- **Streaks are arithmetic, not evidence.** A genuinely good 60% strategy still prints 4–5 losses in a row, routinely, every ~100 trades. A losing streak does not mean the system broke. Only batches of 20+ trades contain information; n=1 contains none.
- `trading/trades.md`: every fill names the plan level it executed, or `unplanned`. **That column is the grade — plan-adherence only.** Ignore the P&L of any single row. The market gives loud, fast, emotional feedback on outcomes and silent feedback on process — this column is how you force yourself to learn from the right channel.

## Part 4 — Short-put addendum

The cash-secured put was chosen *because* it front-loads every decision to entry — know what that means for you:

- **All decisions are already made.** Strike = a price I am genuinely happy to own at. Premium = already collected. Expiry = the calendar decides, not me. There is no stop to get shaken out of, because "what if it drops?" was answered before I clicked.
- **The bad week is coming — that's a schedule, not a risk.** Sometime in the position's life, the stock will have a violent red week, IV will spike, and the put will mark against me −$1,500, −$3,000 on paper. The screen will scream. **That is the panic-exit moment wearing different clothes.** Panic-closing a short put in a vol spike is the same mistake as dumping a stock at flat on a war headline. The answer is pre-written: nothing, unless the invalidation list triggered.
- **Paper marks on a short put are not losses.** The position resolves at expiry, on the stock price vs. the strike — not on the scariest mid-life mark.
- **Assignment is not failure — it's my limit order filling.** I wrote that order on purpose, at a price my own analysis called cheap, and I was paid to place it. If I wouldn't be happy owning at the strike, the fix is *don't sell that put* — not "sell it and hope."
- **Check on the trade's clock:** weekly, plus the dates on the calendar (earnings days above all). Not headlines. A six-month position does not have a minutes-timeframe.
- **The premium's yield is rent on real risk.** High IV pays me because the tail is real, not because someone mispriced it. Size every put so that assignment at the strike — during a crash, which is exactly when assignment happens — is a position I can hold without checking at 11pm.

## Part 5 — Pocket lines

- Calm is a sizing decision, not a personality trait.
- Exits belong to the plan, not the amygdala.
- News must touch the thesis to touch the position.
- The stop is insurance I already paid for — don't jump out the window as well.
- One trade's outcome is noise. The edge only shows over volume.
- Followed plan and lost = good trade. Broke plan and won = worst trade.
- Deserved losses are food costs, not mistakes.
- If it's not good enough to be a rule, it's not good enough to do once.
- Every exception teaches my brain the plan is negotiable. The precedent costs more than the loss.
- A plan is a letter from my best self to my worst self. Right now, I might be the worst self. Trust the letter.
- Be the casino. Never change the rules of roulette over one spin.
- Which market am I in? Route the alarm before obeying it.
- The exit that wasn't in the plan is wrong even when it wins.
